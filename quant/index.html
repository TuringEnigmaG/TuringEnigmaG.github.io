Momentum, tested honestly

I built a research-to-paper-trading system for China A-shares and US equities. Most ideas I tested failed walk-forward validation, and I dropped them. One survived: weekly momentum rotation across 18 Chinese sector ETFs. It now trades on paper every day, run by scheduled cloud agents.

Sep–Oct 2026 · solo project · paper trading only, no real money

Out-of-sample = the second half of the data (Mar 2024 – Oct 2026), which played no part in choosing the strategy. Benchmark = equal-weight buy-and-hold of the same 18 ETFs.

The strategy

  • Universe. 18 Chinese sector and theme ETFs: semiconductors, banks, brokers, coal, liquor, new-energy vehicles and others.
  • Signal. Risk-adjusted momentum over 20, 60 and 120 trading days, plus a 60-day trend filter. An ETF in a downtrend can't be bought, however strong its recent bounce.
  • Portfolio. Hold the top 3, at most 40% each. Cash is allowed: when the trend filter rejects everything, the strategy sits out.
  • Risk. −8% stop-loss per position, executed at the next open.
  • Rebalance. On the last trading day of each week, using the official exchange calendar.

Making the backtest hard to fool

Most backtests look good because they quietly assume things that can't happen in real trading. I removed the ones I could find:

  • No look-ahead. Signals use the close; orders fill at the next day's open.
  • Real costs. Commissions, 5 bp slippage and stamp tax where it applies.
  • Real constraints. China's 100-share lot size, and only buying what the cash on hand can afford.
  • Real calendar. Rebalance days come from official exchange calendars, so holidays shift them correctly.
  • One rule for every idea. Split the history in half. If an idea doesn't beat its benchmark on the second half, it's dropped, however good the first half looks.

Rebalancing frequency decides everything

The same signal gives very different results depending on how often you act on it. Daily rebalancing chases noise: with only 18 candidates, rankings flip constantly and trading costs pile up. Monthly looks best out of sample but was the worst in-sample, which is a sign of instability. Weekly was the only setting that beat the benchmark in both halves.

From backtest to daily paper trading

The paper-trading system reuses the backtest's own functions, so live behavior can't drift from what was validated. Every trading day:

1 · DataPrices after the close, with a check that today's bars are final
2 · SignalSame momentum and trend code as the backtest
3 · PlanNext-day orders written to plan.json
4 · FillNext open, with costs, lot sizes and stop-losses
5 · RecordState and history committed to Git

It runs on scheduled cloud agents: one after the China close, one after the US close, and one that writes a daily report with the next day's plan and news-risk flags. A missed day is replayed automatically on the next run, so a sleeping laptop can't skip a rebalance.

How I worked with Claude Code

I wrote this system with Claude Code as a pair programmer. The split of work was clear:

  • I set the research rules: the out-of-sample gate, which ideas to try, when to drop one, and how much drawdown I'd accept.
  • The agent wrote and ran most of the code: data clients, the backtest engine, the paper-trading loop and the scheduled jobs.
  • We debugged together from logs. Every incident above was diagnosed by reading run logs and data, then fixed and re-run on the exact case that failed.

The core system was working within days. What made it trustworthy was the rule about what counts as evidence, not the speed.

What I took away

  • An honest backtest is mostly a list of things you refused to assume.
  • Parameters that matter, such as rebalancing frequency, should be chosen for stability across periods, not for the best single number.
  • Production surfaces a different class of bugs: stale data, calendars, time zones, network rules. Budget time for them.
