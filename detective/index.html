The Bayesian Detective

Can a model that only counts words solve Agatha Christie's first mystery? I read The Mysterious Affair at Styles (1920) chapter by chapter, scored every sentence that names a suspect, and updated the odds like a detective would.

13 chapters · 7 suspects · ~57,000 words · Python + Bayesian log-odds · full text from Project Gutenberg (public domain in the US)

Who the text points to, chapter by chapter

Each line is the model's probability that a suspect is guilty, using only the evidence up to that chapter. Click a name to highlight it. The slider stops at chapter 12; chapter 13 is Poirot's explanation, which you unlock below.

The verdict

The murderers are Alfred Inglethorp and his accomplice Evelyn Howard. So how did the word-counting detective do?

Caught: Alfred Inglethorp

The model names him from chapter 1 and his probability climbs to by chapter 12. Christie's famous double bluff is to make the obvious suspect look cleared by an alibi. The model can't read alibis, so it never lets go of him.

Missed: Evelyn Howard

She's mentioned times, about as often as the others, yet by chapter 12 she's the least suspicious person in the book (). Christie hides her by keeping suspicious words away from her name.

The red herrings work on readers through plot rather than vocabulary: John Cavendish is arrested in chapter 10, but the words near his name barely move his odds. Dr. Bauerstein peaks in chapter 10, the chapter where he's arrested as a spy.

The takeaway: counting words gets you the obvious suspect and is immune to clever logic, but it can't see what an author deliberately keeps quiet. That's the gap a model that reasons over the plot has to close, which is the next experiment: can an LLM name both killers at chapter 12?

How it works

1 · Find the suspects

Each suspect is matched under every name the book uses ("Mr. Inglethorp", "Alfred"…), while excluding the victim, Mrs. Inglethorp. Pronouns aren't resolved, so "he" and "she" don't count.

2 · Score each sentence

In every sentence that names a suspect, I count incriminating words (poison, strychnine, coffee, the will, quarrel, lied, motive…) and clearing words (alibi, innocent, impossible…). Clearing words count 1.5×.

3 · Update the odds

Each chapter's net evidence, scaled by chapter length, is added to a suspect's log-odds (λ = 0.6). A softmax turns log-odds into probabilities that sum to 1 across the seven suspects. Everyone starts equal.

4 · Be honest about the limits

  • Word co-occurrence isn't accusation: "Alfred was not near the coffee" still counts.
  • The narrator, Hastings, is often wrong, and the model inherits his bias.
  • One novel is an anecdote, not a benchmark.
