The peeking trap

Stopping the moment p dips under 0.05 feels efficient, and it's one of the most common ways A/B tests go wrong. To see it, pick "A/A test: identical strategies" above and press "Run, but stop at the first p < 0.05". There is no real difference, yet if you watch closely enough, chance will hand you a "significant" result far more than 5% of the time.

How it works

The game

  • 12 players: 4 wolves, 4 villagers, seer, witch, hunter, guard. Wolves win by eliminating all villagers or all gods.
  • Every night: the guard protects someone (not the same player twice), wolves kill, the witch may save or poison, the seer checks one player. Guarded and saved on the same night still dies.
  • Every day: the seer may reveal checks, a wolf may counter-claim, then everyone votes.

The people

  • Each good player has a noisy private read of everyone; wolves "feel" wolfier by a set amount (the read-skill slider).
  • A believed seer's checks shift those reads strongly. When two seers claim, each good player picks one to trust.
  • Wolves know each other and vote as a block, targeting the revealed seer first.

The statistics

  • Win rates with Wald 95% intervals.
  • Two-proportion z-test for B − A, with the same seed per arm so both see similar deals.
  • Sample size for 80% power at α = 0.05, from the observed rates.

Honest limits

  • It's a toy: real players bluff, read tone and adapt. The point is the experimental method, not a solved game.
  • Effects depend on the assumptions. The fair-game baseline (≈48% good) was tuned to the read-skill setting.
